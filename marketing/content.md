## Positioning comes first

A brand needs market fit the way a product does: a specific audience
instantly understands what you stand for, wants more of it, and can't easily
get it elsewhere. A brand is **future demand**: most of your audience isn't
buying this month, and the brand decides whom they think of when they are.
Direct-response work captures current demand; both are needed, and only
the second is easy to measure. A brand also moves the business maths —
more reach and warmer leads lower acquisition cost; trust raises what
customers pay and how long they stay. Most brands stand next to fifty similar
competitors and
wonder why nobody notices. Before any content, SHOULD answer in writing:

- **What do we stand for?** The idea, belief or promise the brand is built on.
- **Who exactly is it for?** One specific person, not a market.
- **Why would they care?** What they get from us that they can't get
  elsewhere — the specific problem we solve.
- **What should they feel** when they see the brand? Pick one or two: smart
  ("I learn something every time"), early, belonging, confident, inspired,
  rebellious.
- **How do they like to consume content** (format, platform, length)?
- **What must we be associated with**, so that when they have the problem
  they think of us first? A brand teaches those associations on purpose;
  without that, the audience makes them for you.

Then:

- **Own a narrow category.** The strongest brands are the only one in their
  category, not the best in a crowded one. Common routes: combine two worlds,
  be first and execute, or build around one specific pain point. If no
  difference comes to mind, the problem is the product, not the branding.
  Different also has to be **better in a way that matters** to the buyer
  (Ayo Omojola).
- **Name the real alternative** — often a spreadsheet, a manual process or
  doing nothing — and the "foil" you replace; open with the shift in the
  world, the old game and the new (April Dunford, Andy Raskin).
- **Decided: narrow.** Byron Sharp's research on (mostly consumer) brands
  argues tight targeting caps growth and that being distinctive matters
  more than being differentiated. His evidence is better than the
  practitioners' but comes from mass-market brands with ad budgets; a
  three-person studio with niche products grows by being the obvious choice
  for one kind of buyer. Revisit if a product reaches a mass market.
- **Narrow on the buyer and the transformation, not on the topic.**
  **Decided:** one copywriter argues against "niching" — talk about anything
  that helps the audience get from where they are to where they want to be.
  That fits the narrow rule: the *who* and the *outcome* stay narrow; the
  topics can range across everything on the way there, and the pillars sit
  inside that path.
- **Make the audience the hero.** Every piece answers: what does the reader
  get for their attention? Content earns influence three ways *(practitioner
  framework)*: showing you can do what they want (skill, results, network),
  giving step-by-step directions that work — trust is belief with evidence —
  and showing you share their beliefs and values.
- **Same story everywhere.** Format changes per channel; the story, the
  product's use and the feeling don't.
- **Names:** suggestive beats descriptive — a descriptive name boxes you in
  when the product grows (Arielle Jackson; David Placek, who sells naming).
- **Press** is a credibility link for sales and hiring emails more than a
  signup channel in B2B. Decide what it is for, then pitch named reporters on
  that beat with something for their readers.

The answers belong in the context file agents read
([ai/agents.md](../ai/agents.md#context-before-agents)). Grow the
audience that buys, not views: reach on the wrong people is a vanity metric.

## Founder account and brand account

A company SHOULD run both, with different jobs:

| | Founder account | Brand account |
|---|---|---|
| Voice | First person: opinions, stories, personality | Third person |
| Content | Why we're building this, what I believe, what I'm learning, behind the scenes | Launches, updates, campaigns, community |
| Strength | Builds trust fastest — people follow people | Scales; better product distribution; worth more if the business is sold |
| Risk | The brand depends on one person; a bad personal take hurts it | Harder to feel relatable |

- Show the real journey, not only wins: honest takes, what you're
  struggling with, what you're building. A brand that only wins reads as
  scripted. Showing your face widens the formats you can use and builds
  trust faster; faceless brands are harder to grow.
- **Fuel for the founder account:** once a month, list the questions buyers
  raised most on last month's sales calls, in their words; each one becomes
  a post. The same transcripts feed outbound copy
  ([outbound.md](outbound.md)).

## Original insight is the moat

Content worth following says something only this brand can. Sources, roughly
from hardest to easiest to copy:

- proprietary data (your own numbers, usage, results);
- first-hand experience (what happened on real projects);
- access (people, places, situations others can't reach);
- frameworks (a named way of thinking about a recurring problem);
- contrarian takes, when they are honestly held and argued.

Same principle as the "one element the top results don't have" rule for
search pages
([seo/content.md](../seo/content.md#choosing-what-to-write-practitioner-method)).

## Packaging

Packaging — the title, thumbnail, hook or first line — is a separate skill
from the content itself. The same idea can flop or travel depending on how
it is framed. Good packaging:

- invites curiosity;
- promises a clear payoff, and the content delivers it;
- is specific;
- matches the platform's conventions.

**Search is different:** for a page meant to rank, the `<title>` and `<h1>`
summarise the page without exaggeration
([seo/appearance.md](../seo/appearance.md)). Curiosity-led framing is for
social posts, videos and newsletters, never a reason to bend a search title.

Copy that works is concise, easy to understand, and carries one strong idea.
Keep a swipe file of pieces that worked, with a line on *why*, and use it to
train taste — your own and your agents' (voice examples in
[ai/agents.md](../ai/agents.md#context-before-agents)).

### Copy rules

The single home for copy tells — page, email, ad and post copy all use it.

- **The swap test:** a line that would work unchanged on a competitor's
  site gets rewritten, or flagged `[NEED: differentiator]`.
- **Fix tells from the facts, not with a synonym.** If proof is missing,
  mark `[NEED: proof]`; never invent a number to replace "industry-leading".
- **A claim you can't support names its unlock:** why it falls short, the
  closest claim you can support, and the evidence that would allow the
  original (a review export, a written guarantee).
- **DON'T write the tells of generated copy:**
  - genre words: unlock, seamless, elevate, effortless, robust, powerful,
    cutting-edge, all-in-one, "X, reimagined", "Welcome to";
  - contrast reveals ("It's not X, it's Y", "Not because X. Because Y.") —
    state Y with its reason;
  - negation lists ("No X, no Y, no Z") — say what happens; state one real
    absence once, next to the call to action;
  - self-answered questions ("The result?"), colon reveals ("The best part:
    …"), trailing clauses that only restate;
  - three of everything — at most one list of three and one fragment per
    section;
  - em dashes in headlines, ads, posts and subject lines; one or two per page
    in long copy;
  - "Get started" / "Learn more", and headlines that need a colon.
- **Posts:** no one-sentence-per-line "broetry", no engagement bait ("Agree?",
  "Let that sink in"), no emoji bullets.

### Thinking before writing

Copy is the thinking, then the words *(practitioner method)*. Before any
page, post or ad, answer six questions:

1. **People** — exactly who. The same product needs a different message for
   each kind of buyer (a first-time runner fears not finishing; a runner
   over 40 fears injury). Map their daily reality, frustrations, fears and
   goals, in their own words from calls and reviews; don't start writing
   until there is almost too much to say.
2. **Positioning** — why you over anyone else. Often a fact everyone in the
   category has and nobody says; the first to say it owns it. AI won't find
   these; you have to look.
3. **Promise** — the outcome, not the feature. Ask "so what?" until the
   answer is about who the customer becomes.
4. **Proof** — what you could *point at* if you couldn't speak: logos, a
   number, "400 clients, 11 years". Client results beat your own; proof
   from people like the reader beats proof from anyone else.
5. **Priority** — why now. Cost of staying put works best; scarcity and
   deadlines only when real ([conversion.md](conversion.md#pricing-pages)).
6. **Process** — how it works: the mechanism. "Most people believe X; it's
   wrong because Y; the answer is Z."

**Where the reader is** sets the language. Awareness runs unaware →
problem-aware → solution-aware → product-aware → most aware: name the
problem for the first, explain options for the next, show why yours is
different, then just make the offer. **How crowded the market is** sets the
claim: first in the market, state it; others copied it, make it bigger;
claims worn out, introduce a mechanism; mechanism copied, improve it; nobody
believes claims any more, lead with identity and a person. To find the
stage, read 20 competitor ads in the public ad libraries and write down
each claim; if they all say the same thing, you need a mechanism.

A buyer needs three beliefs before buying: that the method works, that
*they* can do it, and that it will work in their real life. Turn every
unaddressed belief into an angle.

### Writing it

- **Clear, concise, concrete, conversational, with cadence.** Remove what's
  unclear rather than adding explanation ("There's a fire downstairs. Open
  the window."). Treat every word as costing money. Make it something the
  reader can picture ("from 14 leads a month to 70", not "we help businesses
  grow"). Write how the reader talks. Vary sentence length; read it aloud
  and rewrite where you stumble.
- **The hook carries most of the result.** It works when it promises a
  benefit, applies to many readers, gives a reason to listen to you, and
  looks easy to read and act on. **Describe a moment, not a category**:
  "Do you turn the brightness down when you open your bank app?" beats "Are
  you broke?" Before shipping a hook: is it a callout or an open loop? Would
  the client say it aloud to a friend? Would a stranger with no context
  understand it? Is it free of insider words? Can you picture it?
- **Body:** what → why → how for teaching; "but … therefore …" for stories
  (tension, not "and then … and then").
- **One call to action:** who it's for, what to do, by when, how. Several
  options lower the chance of any.
- **Tell the truth.** Change reality until the claim is true; never stretch
  the claim. Admitting a real weakness ("I can't dance; I can write") makes
  the strength believable.

## Funnel and pillars

Every piece of organic content has **one job**: reach new people, build
trust, or convert.

| Stage | Job | Formats |
|---|---|---|
| Top | Get discovered | Broad, shareable: takes, trends, short clips |
| Middle | Turn followers into people who trust you | How-tos, long form, tutorials, case studies, behind the scenes |
| Bottom | Turn trust into customers | Demos, customer results, offers and launches, free resources that lead to paid ones, answers to objections |

- The mix depends on the business: a news account is almost all top of
  funnel, a product page almost all bottom. Decide the split; don't let it
  happen. DON'T neglect the top — it is what keeps a brand growing.
- **Content pillars:** the 3–5 topics the brand is known for. A good pillar
  sits where three things overlap: what the audience cares about, what you
  are credible on, and what leads naturally to what you sell. Each pillar
  gets top, middle and bottom pieces, packaged per platform.

## Channels

- **Few platforms, done properly.** A good idea on the wrong platform still
  flops. Pick two or three where the audience actually is and learn how each
  one decides what to show. Long-form video skills transfer well to short
  form.
- **One channel, one job:** search answers questions, social starts
  conversations, email arrives at a specific moment. The story stays the same
  (positioning above); posting the identical message on five channels pays
  five times for one impression.
- **Hold a cadence**: fixed times to write, film and batch. Consistency beats
  bursts.
- **Rent to find, own to keep.** Social platforms rent you an audience;
  algorithms change and accounts get restricted overnight. Use them to find
  people, and move people to owned channels (newsletter, email list,
  community) to keep them. SHOULD start the newsletter on day one, even
  before publishing regularly.
- **Two tests, two tools.** Test *creative* organically: post several
  hooks, put money only behind what already travelled. Test a *channel* with
  a small paid budget, because organic reach says nothing about whether a
  paid audience exists there. **Decided:** operators split between
  "organic first" and "a little paid spend first" (Timothy Davis); they are
  answering different questions, so both are kept with their jobs named.
- **X specifically:** its ranking (published code) scores replies, shares by
  DM, follows and profile clicks separately and subtracts predicted mutes and
  blocks — write for those; space original posts apart (reported).
- **Repurpose from a hall of fame.** Save every post that beats your
  average (title, link, date, reposted yet?). Then, *(practitioner)*:
  **repost** winners after about 90 days — most of the audience never saw
  them; **repurpose** the idea for each platform, reformatted, not pasted;
  **remix** it — the same idea in a new form, or the same form with a new
  idea. Text-only platforms test the *idea* cleanly, with no visual to
  confound the result.

## Community

An audience follows you; a community belongs to something. To build one:

- **Make membership mean something:** a name, shared language and beliefs,
  a visual identity, public recognition, and a way in that takes some
  qualifying.
- **Give it a home** that fits the audience: a real-time chat for active
  groups, an announcements channel for fast updates, a course platform for
  education brands, a simple private group for a low barrier. A newsletter is
  the best entry point but not a community by itself.
- **Stay close:** the founder answers questions and shares behind-the-scenes
  updates there, in person.
- **Create rituals:** weekly events, challenges, member spotlights, an
  annual event or meetup.

## Creators

- **MUST disclose every paid or gifted creator post** where viewers see it
  (#ad in text, said aloud in video) plus the platform's paid-partnership
  label; the brand is liable too. Write disclosure into the agreement and
  check it shipped. Never pay for undisclosed comments. (US FTC rules as
  summarised; check each country's.)
- Brief with the why, two or three talking points, the call to action and
  the limits — never a word-for-word script. Agree paid-ad usage rights up
  front (commonly 3–6 months). Codes, tracked links and the "how did you
  hear about us" question ([conversion.md](conversion.md#measurement)) ready
  before launch.

## Testing creative

- **Ground creative in a corpus:** winning ads, 50–100 customer reviews, ad
  comments. Each concept cites where it came from; with no corpus, stop and
  gather one.
- Ask for genuinely different concepts (a worked example, a customer story,
  a raw demo, a founder take), not fifty headlines on one idea. Claims about
  how ad platforms group similar ads are a practitioner's and unverified; the
  practice holds regardless.
- **Hypotheses that can lose**, in the format in
  [conversion.md](conversion.md#experiments). A headline that wins on
  click-through is exploratory until a landing-page test confirms it.
- **Read the funnel before changing an ad:** weak stop rate → the opening
  visual; weak hold → seconds 3–15; weak click-through → the promise; weak
  conversion → the landing page doesn't match. Change one part per round.
- **Judge with enough spend:** at least ~3× the target cost per qualified
  lead before calling an ad; zero qualified leads at that spend means drop
  the concept (one practitioner's threshold, from Poisson arithmetic — 2×
  misses about one good ad in eight). Keep ~20% of budget for tests.
- DON'T edit a running ad — it resets learning; launch a new one beside it.
  Raise budgets by at most ~20% every few days, and don't rebuild campaigns
  often — give delivery time to learn.
- **Optimise for lifetime gross profit, not cost per lead.** In one
  practitioner's test, the cheap-lead campaign brought buyers worth about a
  tenth of the dearer one (their numbers).
- **Build variations from parts:** one ad = a title, about five hooks, one
  body, a few calls to action, combined; double down on the branch that
  works. Plan enough distinct variations that creative doesn't tire as
  spend grows. On broad targeting, **the copy does the targeting** — say who
  it's for and the platform finds them *(practitioner; the platform's
  mechanics are not documented here)*.
- AI actors are fine for testing scripts; pay a real person to run the
  winner — it performs better and keeps platform labelling rules simple.
- **UGC creators:** pick by views ÷ followers on recent videos, not by
  follower count. Small creators whose videos travel far past their own
  audience are the ones to brief. Watch their work before reaching out, then
  brief them with what the data showed and let them adapt it.

## Building a content team

- Early on one person covers several roles (strategy and voice, writing,
  design, video, community). Split them as volume grows; strategy, voice and
  the quality bar stay with one owner.
- **Hire for taste and thinking, train the skill.** Content is coachable but
  context-heavy; nobody arrives knowing the brand.
- SHOULD run a **paid test task** before hiring
  ([operations/agency.md](../operations/agency.md#hiring)).
- Good candidates are often already posting: small accounts whose thinking
  fits the brand. Be public about the role.
- Be wary of someone leading content for the first time with no prior work
  next to a content lead. If you can't judge content yourself, borrow the
  judgement of someone who has hired for it.
- What makes the role attractive: stable pay, real creative control, a team
  rather than a one-person department, public credit, and room for their own
  brand.
- **Every recurring process gets a short written SOP** (research, hand-offs,
  who owns what). Draft it from meeting notes; a plain doc is enough.
- Set expectations on budget early: one content hire cannot be a whole
  marketing department.
